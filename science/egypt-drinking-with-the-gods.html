The Origins of Alcohol · Episode 1.3

Egypt: Drinking with the Gods

Mindrink ScienceEpisode 1.3Research-led history

Pleasure, warnings and sacred intoxication in ancient Egypt

In a fourteenth-century BC tomb painting at Thebes, a woman offers the sculptor Nebamun a bowl of wine. “Drink and make a happy day!” the inscription invites him. Surrounded by his family, he receives a drink that belongs among the pleasures of the life he wanted to preserve after death.[1]

Egyptian scribes could sound much less welcoming, warning of humiliation, injury and neglected duties. Yet a royal inscription names a portico of drunkenness dedicated to a goddess, while a song expresses a desire for intoxication in her honour.[9][10][12] How could people embrace alcohol as part of a desirable life, recognise its dangers, and deliberately seek drunkenness in worship?

Much of this evidence comes from the tombs and temples around Thebes, near modern Luxor, during the New Kingdom, approximately 1550–1070 BC. Around the offered bowl were other people, with invitations and expectations of their own; beyond the pleasures of company lay the question of what becoming drunk might cost, or make possible.

Alcohol as part of a desirable life

Beer as an everyday provision

During the Middle Kingdom, around 1980 BC, a miniature bakery and brewery was placed in the tomb of the royal steward Meketre at Thebes—about six centuries before Nebamun's banquet was painted. Inside a wooden building less than a metre long, painted figures crush grain, grind it and work beside vats and jars. Bread and beer are made in adjoining rooms.[2]

Before anyone could offer a bowl, someone had to prepare the grain, tend the mixture and fill the vessels. The model was meant to ensure that such work would continue for Meketre after death. Beer belonged beside bread among the provisions worth securing for eternity. Having enough to drink mattered here; wanting to become drunk was a different question.

A source of pleasure

For the pleasures of drinking in company, the fifteenth-century BC tomb of Paheri at Elkab, farther up the Nile, offers a livelier picture. The Egyptologist John Baines notes that rank is marked by the figures' size and seating: privileged guests sit on chairs, others on mats, in groups separated by gender. Some are identified as deceased.[8]

Among its female guests, Amen-sat turns down wine. An attendant encourages her to celebrate and drink until she is drunk. Another guest asks for eighteen cups and declares her wish to become drunk: “my inside is as dry as straw!” Elsewhere, a companion urges a reluctant drinker not to spoil the entertainment.[3]

These exchanges were composed for a tomb, not overheard at a party; the eighteen cups are a request, not a tally of drinks consumed. With musicians, a dancer and women clapping around them, the guests are shown responding differently to the offered wine.[3] Hospitality shades into pressure when an attendant persists or a companion joins the persuasion. Refusal has a place in the scene, but accepting a drink can be presented as part of joining in the pleasure.

Within that company, the woman asking for eighteen cups needs no encouragement. Becoming drunk is part of what she looks forward to, alongside the music and entertainment. Where Meketre's model promises a supply of beer, Paheri's banquet makes room for the appetite for wine and its effects.

Drinking together

The preparations for another gathering survive at Deir el-Medina, the village of the workers who built and decorated royal tombs near Thebes. An inscription on several joined fragments records contributions to a drinking party associated with Hathor, in the reign of Ramesses III or IV, in the twelfth century BC.[5][15] A woman named Isis appears at the beginning of the record. At least thirty-three women and three men are named as contributors, often giving five or ten loaves of bread.[15]

The occasion remains uncertain. The name Hathor could refer to the goddess or to a woman being celebrated; Heidi Jauhiainen favours the latter reading.[4][15] The list records contributions, not confirmed attendance or how much anyone drank. But beside the invitations painted for Paheri, these names and loaves bring another aspect of drinking into view: people helping to provide for a shared gathering.

The unsettling effects of intoxication

Drinking in company created expectations as well as pleasures. Paheri's guests could be urged to take another bowl, but what happened when the effects became less agreeable? Some banquet painters included those moments too, while writers of advice made them reasons to beware of excess.

The visible effects of excess

In the Theban tomb of Amenemhat, dated to about 1479–1425 BC, a guest vomits into a jar. The Metropolitan Museum's catalogue Drink and Be Merry! identifies the scene as the aftermath of excessive alcohol consumption.[7]

Sickness might seem an obvious way to warn against drinking, but Baines offers a different reading of a similar scene in Djeserkareseneb's tomb, from around 1400 BC. There, a man vomits into a vessel provided for the purpose. Such behaviour, Baines argues, could be accepted on that occasion, and its depiction might even have been humorous.[8] On this reading, the painter acknowledges the body's less graceful response to drink without turning a celebration of company into a condemnation of it.

Judgment and dignity

The company around a drinker looks less forgiving in the Instruction of Ani. Its author warns against too much beer: discreditable words may escape without the speaker's awareness, he may fall and injure himself, and no one will offer a hand. Even his drinking companions distance themselves.[9]

The warning is as much about vulnerability as bad behaviour. Set beside the encouragement on Paheri's walls, it exposes an uncomfortable possibility: the people who make drinking attractive may offer little help when its pleasures turn to distress. Speech, bodily control and one's standing among companions can all be lost together.

Ani's author chooses an alarming outcome to show his reader how to behave. The surviving Boulaq manuscript dates to the Twenty-first Dynasty, roughly the eleventh–tenth centuries BC, after the New Kingdom banquet scenes. The work itself is thought to have been composed earlier.[9]

Responsibilities neglected

A pupil who has abandoned his writing for pleasure is rebuked in Papyrus Anastasi IV, copied around 1200 BC. He wanders about reeking of beer. The comparison with a boat whose steering gear is defective makes his failing plain: he is no longer directing himself as he should.[10]

In the same literary rebuke, the speaker also denounces wine and urges the pupil to give up drink, not merely to drink less while working.[10] The problem is more than an undignified appearance. Drinking has displaced the discipline expected of a future scribe; what Paheri's eager guest anticipates as pleasure is presented here as a way to squander one's abilities.

The banquet painter and the writers of advice give excess different weight. A sick guest may remain part of the humour of a gathering; Ani's author makes injury and abandonment reasons to fear it, while the pupil is told to change his ways. These warnings reveal what their authors thought was at stake in drunkenness: the ability to speak and act with judgment, to rely on one's companions and to fulfil one's obligations.

The sacred significance of intoxication

In those warnings, losing command of oneself threatens dignity and responsibility. The religious evidence adds a different reason for wanting to become drunk.

Intoxication saves humanity

In the story usually called The Destruction of Mankind, the sun god Ra has grown old and humanity rebels against him. He sends his daughter Hathor to punish the rebels. When he wishes to spare those who remain, he has beer mixed with red ochre and spread over the land. The goddess drinks the blood-coloured liquid until she is intoxicated, and the killing stops.[11] The loss of control feared in the scribal warnings has a saving purpose in this story.

The episode belongs to the Book of the Heavenly Cow, whose fullest surviving version was inscribed in Seti I's tomb in the thirteenth century BC. Its events take place in divine time, as part of an explanation of the separation of gods and humans and the reordering of the cosmos.[11] It gives drunkenness a religious meaning, rather than describing a human drinking ceremony.

A sacred place for drunkenness

A royal dedication brings us from divine stories to a temple at Thebes. Around the 1470s BC, Hatshepsut made a columned “portico of drunkenness” for Mut, according to Betsy Bryan's translation of its inscription.[12] The words survive on column fragments found at Mut's temple at Karnak. Intoxication appears here in a religious dedication, not as an accusation of disgrace.

A Ptolemaic festival calendar describes red-tinted beer cheering Mut's heart and drawing her out of anger.[12] It recalls the Hathor story, in which beer also changes a goddess's behaviour. But these sources span more than a millennium: Hatshepsut's portico predates the surviving copies of the myth, and the calendar is much later still. Their parallels do not establish what ceremonies took place in the portico or whether they re-enacted the myth.[11][12]

Religious drinking together

The dedication names drunkenness; a tomb song gives voice to people who wanted it. Welcoming Mut's return in an early-fourteenth-century BC tomb at Thebes, the song describes her sistrum players as “desiring drunkenness from a goblet of gold,” in Bryan's translation.[12]

The sistrum was a ritual rattle, played by shaking it.[14] In this tomb song, the musicians' wish to become drunk accompanies invitations to adorn oneself and rejoice as the goddess returns.

Bryan interprets such songs as evidence that people sought encounters with a goddess and, in funerary celebrations, with the dead through intoxication.[12] On this reading, becoming drunk offered the possibility of approaching beings beyond everyday human reach.

Music and shared drink could bring the pleasures of company to worship. Yet we hear far less from those who brewed the beer and carried the bowls than from those who commissioned tombs or wrote instructions. The Deir el-Medina list brings some village contributors into view, but these sources cannot speak for everyone who made, served or shared a drink.

Living with a powerful drink

Beer could be a provision worth securing for eternity, and wine a pleasure to share, without drunkenness being welcomed everywhere. Banquet scenes could accommodate excess, while advice literature warned of injury, abandonment and neglected duties.

In Bryan's reading of the religious songs, intoxication offered the prospect of drawing closer to a goddess or the dead. The change in consciousness was not merely a risk to tolerate while celebrating; it could itself be the purpose. That hope did not erase the dangers described by the scribes. It gave a reason to seek the very effects that made drunkenness troubling elsewhere.
