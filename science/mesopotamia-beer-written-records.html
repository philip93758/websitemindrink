The Origins of Alcohol · Episode 1.2

Mesopotamia: What the First Written Records Tell Us About Beer

Mindrink ScienceEpisode 1.2Research-led history

Beer in the age of the first cities and the first scribes

Throughout prehistory, fermented drinks are known to us only through what they left behind: residues at the bottom of a vessel, wear on a stone. No one described them, because no one wrote. In Mesopotamia, for the first time, those who drink beer begin to leave documents — and those documents change the very nature of what we can know.

Mesopotamia is the land between the Tigris and the Euphrates, in what is now Iraq. It was in the southern part of this land — a hot plain where farming depended on irrigation — that some of the world's first large cities took shape.

A concentration of people on this scale presupposes an agricultural surplus: enough grain to feed those who do not farm — artisans, priests, scribes — and great institutions to collect and redistribute grain, herds, textiles and labour. Redistribution on that scale creates a practical problem: how to keep track of what enters the stores, what leaves them, and who receives what. One person's memory is no longer sufficient.

Writing appears in this administrative setting. The oldest surviving documents, dated to around 3200 to 3000 BC, are neither narratives nor poems: they are accounts.[2][3] Their signs, drawn or impressed into moist clay, were used first to record quantities, goods and categories rather than to transcribe speech sound by sound — a system specialists call proto-cuneiform. It is not an alphabet. Over the following centuries, its wedge-shaped signs — cuneiform proper — came to record words and syllables, then correspondence, hymns, myths and laws.[3]

This shift changes the historian's position. In Episode 1.1, the earliest fermented drinks were inferred from traces: altered starch grains in a mortar, acids absorbed into the wall of a jar. Interpretation did all the work. In Mesopotamia, a new kind of source appears alongside those clues: people begin to write.

And among the things the first administrators judged worth counting was beer.[2]

Beer enters the accounts

In the oldest tablets, beer does not appear as a novelty requiring explanation. It is already classified: variants of a sign resembling a jar correspond to nine categories interpreted as different kinds of beer, with ingredients counted separately, including products identified as malt and coarsely ground grain.[2] The precise meaning of these signs remains beyond reach, but the distinction is already there. The administration even had its conventions: on the archaic tablets studied by Salvatore Monaco, the notation of quantities varied by commodity, so that a practised reader could identify barley or malt from the way the number was written.[4] This is not the fumbling of an administration discovering beer; it is the routine of one already managing it as a well-established product.

A thousand years later, under the Third Dynasty of Ur — around 2100 to 2000 BC — the documents allow us to trace the circuits themselves: the grain that enters the breweries, the beer that emerges. A single large account studied by Peter Damerow records deliveries approaching 300,000 litres and carries forward a deficit from the previous period.[2] Beer is not merely produced in quantity: it has become an accounting obligation, something one can owe. Behind these volumes, people appear — brewers in the service of palaces and temples, several of whom validated their transactions with a personal seal.[5]

It is with the recipients that these archives most clearly contradict a received idea. It is often said that Sumerians were "paid in beer", and the accounts seem at first to confirm it, so much beer circulates within them. But Neumann shows that the ordinary compensation for palace and temple workers was different: barley, distributed each month, supplemented by wool once a year. Beer appears elsewhere, in specific distributions tied to a mission, a rank, a situation — often where it compensates for the lack of a household. An Umma account thus records flour and monthly beer rations for thirty-nine deported women and ten children, who, according to Neumann, received ready-to-consume provisions because they had no household in which to prepare their own meals. A man on the road fell under the same logic.[5]

Around 2050 BC, in Umma, a scribe keeps the accounts of supplies for travellers on official business. On a clay tablet he records what a man named Agu'a received, apparently en route to Elam: bread, oil, three fish, three bunches of onions, good-quality beer, and a jar of a brewing product called dida.[1] In these messenger accounts, the quantities varied with the traveller's function and rank.[5] No one intended to inform us about Agu'a. But because the accounts had to be kept, we know his name, what he drank, and on what grounds — a precision prehistory never supplies.

These documents were nonetheless produced by large institutions, for their own needs. Studying Ur between about 3000 and 2600 BC, the archaeologist Giacomo Benati stresses how invisible independent artisans and villagers remain in the written record.[6] That temples counted their beer does not demonstrate they controlled everything brewed around them.

Even where the figures are precise, they do not describe what they measure: at Lagash, around 2350 BC, Damerow noted monthly grain issues that barely varied for more than ten years, a regularity that resembles an administrative quota more than a record of successive brewings.[2]

This is the limit of these archives. They tell us a great deal about quantities, categories and obligations; they tell us surprisingly little about what was actually in the jar.

What was this beer like?

The answer must be built from the words the accounts use, and those words resist. The simplest case is barley. As with the prehistoric preparations of Episode 1.1, it required treatment: the grain contains mostly starch, which must be broken down into sugars before yeasts can turn them into alcohol. Letting barley germinate produces malt, whose enzymes accomplish part of that work.[2]

The difficulty begins with bappir, commonly translated as "beer bread." The translation immediately conjures an image: a baked loaf, then crumbled into water. It may be correct. But the accounts generally measure bappir by volume, not by the number of loaves — an odd way to count bread.[2] "Beer bread" is therefore a hypothesis disguised as a translation.

On the finished drink, the texts are at once abundant and silent. Documents dated to around 2500 BC distinguish categories rendered as dark beer, dark and sweet, red, or filtered; Ur III accounts commonly speak of ordinary beer and good beer.[2] These words classify without describing. "Good" yields no alcohol strength, and "filtered" does not say how clear the liquid was. Reconstructions that present Mesopotamian beer as a thick, weak drink akin to kvass, or conversely as a strong ale, go further than the sources allow.

Two points are better established. Nothing attests the use of hops, whose bitterness defines so many beers today; that flavour should not be attributed to these.[2][7] And some preparations likely kept cereal particles in suspension — which probably explains the long drinking tubes shown in images, since a tube allows one to draw liquid from below the surface while leaving the residue at the bottom.[2][7] That does not mean every beer was cloudy, or that everyone drank this way.

There is still the text often presented as the solution: the Hymn to Ninkasi, a Sumerian poem addressed to the goddess of brewing, known from copies written around 1800 BC. It enumerates actions — dough, oven, malt, vessels — and the sequence resembles a process. But it is a hymn, not a manual: it does not say when malt and bappir were combined, and Damerow even doubts that the operations described follow the order of a complete brewing.[2][9] A more insidious difficulty arises: modern translations use words such as malt, wort, or mashing, which belong to the technical vocabulary of contemporary brewing. To read the hymn in our languages is already to read it through a modern brewery. Lacking a recipe, people have therefore tried brewing: experiments based on much later installations have shown that brewing in large, partly buried jars was technically possible, without thereby recovering a "Sumerian beer."[2]

We are left with a curious asymmetry: accountants able to distinguish and quantify several kinds of beer with remarkable precision, yet historians unable to say with certainty what a single one tasted like. Knowing what was in the jar does not, moreover, tell us what drinking meant to those who held it.

Shared, sung about, regulated

The drink first becomes visible in images. A lapis-lazuli cylinder seal found in the tomb of Puabi, in the Royal Cemetery at Ur, around 2600 BC, contains two banquet scenes within a few centimetres of height. Rolled on clay, it prints two registers of figures: above, seated guests drink through long tubes from a large vessel; below, others hold cups while servants attend them.[8] The shared jar is generally associated with beer, while the cups could contain wine, and one cannot identify each drink. But one thing is unmistakable: the difference between those who sit and those who serve. The banquet is shared, yet it remains hierarchical.

Such an object comes from a richly furnished tomb, a reminder that Mesopotamian art first shows us the powerful. Yet people drank together far from royal tombs: excavations at Ur have yielded a considerable quantity of mass-produced cup fragments, dated to around 2900 to 2700 BC, from houses, rubbish deposits and graves. Benati reads in this abundance the mark of collective consumption, perhaps linked to shared labour — at harvest time, a host able to offer food and beer could gather more hands than a single household could supply.[6]

Court texts share the seal's bias: a hymn of praise shows Shulgi, king of Ur around 2050 BC — roughly Agu'a's time — drinking in a palace with the sun god Utu and the goddess Inanna at his table.[5] The scene is not the record of a meal; the choice of companions chiefly expresses the relationship the king claims with the gods. Beer reached the divine in other ways besides the king's table: it entered offerings and religious ceremonies, and brewing had its own goddess, Ninkasi.[5]

The hymn addressed to her then reveals its full meaning, once one ceases to search it for a recipe. It is a poem. Each pair of verses repeats the goddess's name as a refrain; the details are material — a large shovel, an oven, reed mats, a vessel whose sound is said to be pleasant. And the poem concludes with the beer that flows: "It is you who pour out the filtered beer of the collector vat; it is like the onrush of the Tigris and the Euphrates."[9] The two rivers that made agriculture possible in this plain here serve to describe a liquid being poured. A society does not compose such lines for an indifferent drink.

Literary texts do not ignore the effects. In the Sumerian narrative Inana and Enki, the visiting goddess is received with food, water and beer; Enki drinks with her and cedes divine powers tied to human institutions and skills, including kingship. When the effects wear off, he asks his minister where those powers have gone.[10] The myth does not tell us the strength of ordinary beers, but it shows that intoxication and its consequences were familiar enough to serve as the spring of a plot.

What remained was the place. An Ur III document cited by Neumann mentions a credit contract concluded at a tavern-keeper's.[5] People drank there, and they did business there.

Several centuries later, around 1750 BC, the laws associated with the Babylonian king Hammurabi take an interest in both aspects. A provision, traditionally numbered 108, concerns the payment a tavern-keeper accepts, barley or silver, and the value of the drink served in exchange. The next provides death for the tavern-keeper who fails to arrest people preparing criminal acts in her house and deliver them to the authorities.[11] These are prescribed penalties, not trial records: they tell us what power wanted to regulate, not how people behaved. The object of that concern is nonetheless revealing. A drinking-house had become a place that had to be regulated, where money changed hands and people gathered out of the authorities' sight.

What, in the end, did writing change?

Not certainty. An accountant is not an ethnographer: he records what the administration needs. A hymn is not a manual. A banquet scene is not a census. A law states what power wants to regulate, not what people do. On questions as elementary as the taste or strength of a beer, Mesopotamia leaves us in uncertainty — and it tells us nothing about the invention of the drink, which was being fermented long before its first cities.

Its importance is of another order. For the first time in the history of alcohol, we can follow a drink across the different spheres of a complex society. The same beer is counted by scribes, distributed by institutions, produced by brewers who seal their transactions, shared around a common vessel, engraved on a seal, sung in a poem, offered to the gods, sold in a tavern, and finally regulated by law. None of these views is complete, and each distorts in its own way. But they bear on the same object, and it is that overlap that is new: prehistory offered us only one angle at a time, and always by inference.

The beer itself still partly eludes us. The culture around it no longer does.

Mesopotamia is not alone in having left such a dossier. In the Nile valley, at the same period, another urban society was writing, brewing and keeping accounts.
