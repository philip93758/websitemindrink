The Origins of Alcohol · Episode 1.2

Mesopotamia: What the First Written Records Tell Us About Beer

Mindrink ScienceEpisode 1.2Research-led history

In the twenty-first century BC, a scribe recorded provisions for a man named Agu’a: bread, oil, three fish, three bunches of onions, good-quality beer and a jar of a brewing product called dida. The clay tablet, from the Mesopotamian city of Umma, lists other recipients and ends with the totals. It belongs to a group of accounts recording supplies for messengers and other travellers on official business.[1]

The list was drawn up to keep track of supplies, yet it preserves a glimpse of a traveller’s working life. At the prehistoric sites explored in Episode 1.1, archaeologists had to examine residues and worn stone surfaces to work out what people might have prepared and drunk. With tablets such as this, we can begin to put names to the recipients and understand why they were given a drink.

By Agu’a’s time, writing in southern Mesopotamia was already more than a thousand years old. The earliest surviving accounts, dating to roughly 3200–3000 BC, come from the growing cities of what is now southern Iraq. Agricultural surpluses supported specialised trades, and large institutions collected and distributed grain and other goods. Their accounts recorded what passed through the stores and who received a share.[2][3]

Rather than letters of an alphabet, the first scribes used signs drawn or impressed into moist clay, combining numerical marks with symbols for commodities and other information. This early system, known as proto-cuneiform, developed into the wedge-shaped script used to record words and syllables. Over the following centuries, its uses extended far beyond accounting.[3]

Beer appears among the commodities in the earliest accounts.[2] Later texts would praise its goddess, describe drinking among the gods and prescribe punishments for tavern-keepers. These sources let us ask a different question from the one posed by prehistoric residues: when people began leaving written records, what did they tell us about their beer?

Beer in the accounts

In accounts from the late fourth millennium BC, variations on a sign resembling a jar represent nine categories interpreted as beer. Their precise meanings remain difficult to recover, but scribes were already distinguishing one kind from another. They also listed brewing ingredients separately, including products identified as malt and coarsely ground grain.[2]

Even a number could tell an experienced reader more than a quantity. On the early tablets studied by Salvatore Monaco, scribes wrote numbers differently depending on whether they were recording barley, malt or other grain products. The notation helped identify the ingredient as well as its amount.[4]

Later records make the relationship between the granary and the brewer easier to follow. In the Ur III period, around 2100–2000 BC, when the kings of Ur ruled much of Mesopotamia, accounts registered grain received by breweries and beer delivered in return. One large account examined by the historian Peter Damerow records deliveries approaching 300,000 litres of beer. It also carries forward a deficit from an earlier period: obligations left unmet in one account remained to be settled in the next.[2]

Some brewers can be identified by name. In his study of the Ur III period, Hans Neumann describes their work for palace and temple households; some used personal seals to identify their transactions. Legal documents record brewers buying or selling livestock and enslaved people, evidence that at least some had wealth and standing. Both men and women appear in the wider workforce engaged in beer production.[5]

These accounts complicate the familiar claim that the Sumerians were “paid in beer”. Among the palace and temple workers studied by Neumann, the normal basic ration was barley, issued monthly, with wool distributed once a year. Other arrangements provided different foodstuffs, garments or land for subsistence. Beer generally appeared as an additional allowance for particular needs, not as everyone’s regular pay.[5]

The recipients included officials and messengers on special service, but also women and children working for a temple for only a few days. One account from Umma records flour and monthly beer rations for thirty-nine deported women and ten children. Neumann suggests that they may have needed prepared food and drink because they had no households in which to make their own. An allowance of beer could thus provision an official on business or women and children displaced from home.[5]

Within the messenger accounts, allowances of beer, bread, onions and oil could vary with a traveller’s duties and rank. Agu’a, apparently bound for Elam, to the east of Mesopotamia, belonged to this system. His tablet records one small part of the cost of keeping official business moving.[1][5]

People outside these institutions are harder to find in the accounts. In his study of early third-millennium Ur, the archaeologist Giacomo Benati notes how poorly the written evidence represents independent craftspeople, villagers and other groups. The large archives give us an unusually close view of palace and temple activity, but do not establish that these institutions controlled every drink made or shared in the surrounding settlements.[6]

Nor does an exact quantity necessarily reveal how the beer was made. Looking back to records from Lagash in the twenty-fourth century BC, Damerow found monthly grain allocations that scarcely changed for more than a decade. Their regularity suggests an administrative quota rather than measurements of individual batches. An accountant needed to know whether a brewer had met his obligations; the method of brewing could go unrecorded. Even a precise ratio of grain to beer may tell us more about how the output was valued than what happened inside the vessel.[2]

What was in the vessel?

To imagine the drink, we can begin with the barley named in the accounts. As with the prehistoric drinks considered in Episode 1.1, the grain needed preparation: it contains mainly starch, which must be broken down into sugars that yeasts can ferment. Allowing barley to sprout produces malt, whose enzymes help bring about this change.[2]

Bappir, often translated as “beer bread”, was a processed grain product whose preparation and role in brewing remain disputed. Its name invites us to imagine a loaf crumbled into water, yet the accounts usually measured it by volume, not by the loaf. The tablets also distinguish beer from dida, the product allocated to Agu’a. Damerow suggests that this may have been a concentrate or a preparation that could be stored and later made into beer.[1][2]

The beer itself varied across places and periods. Records from the middle of the third millennium, around 2500 BC, distinguish categories translated as dark, sweet dark, red and strained beer; the later Ur III accounts commonly use terms rendered as ordinary and good. The description “good” does not tell us how much alcohol a beer contained, nor does “strained” tell us how clear the liquid became.[2]

Some preparations may have retained grain particles, making a long drinking tube useful for drawing liquid from below the surface. Such tubes appear in images, though they cannot tell us whether every beer was thick or everyone drank this way. Nor is there evidence for hops in the brewing traditions considered here, so we should not assume the familiar bitterness of many modern beers. Sweetness and acidity could have varied with both the ingredients and the fermentation.[2][7]

At Tall Bazi, in northern Syria, vessels and residues from houses dating to the thirteenth century BC led researchers to investigate brewing in large jars partly sunk into the floor. In an experiment using malt and equipment suited to the archaeological setting, they produced beer through both alcoholic and lactic fermentation, the latter contributing acidity. The result shows that brewing could succeed under those conditions, not that the same method was used centuries earlier in southern Mesopotamia, where the surroundings and brewing traditions might have differed.[2]

Images of drinking offer a different kind of detail, showing the vessels in use and the people gathered around them.

Drinking among people and gods

At Ur, we can look much further back than the houses of Tall Bazi. A lapis lazuli cylinder seal found in Puabi’s tomb in the Royal Cemetery, dating to around 2600 BC, shows two drinking scenes within an image only a few centimetres high. Rolled over clay, its carved surface produces two bands of figures: above, people seated around a large vessel drink through long tubes; below, others hold cups while attendants carry vessels.[8]

The shared jar is generally associated with beer, whereas the cups could also have held wine. We cannot identify every drink, but we can distinguish the seated guests from those serving them. Found in a richly furnished tomb, the seal presents a shared banquet in which distinctions of rank remain visible.[8]

Older finds from Ur include large numbers of fragments of mass-produced goblets, dating to around 2900–2700 BC, recovered from houses, rubbish deposits and graves. Benati interprets their abundance as evidence for shared drinking and suggests a connection with collective work. At busy times such as the harvest, a host offering food and beer might have brought together more workers than one household could supply. Whether those drinking together were also working together cannot be established from the vessels alone.[6]

A hymn celebrating Shulgi, a king of Ur who ruled in the twenty-first century BC, gives the banquet a more exalted setting. The king drinks beer in a palace alongside the sun god Utu, with singers, drums and the goddess Inanna at the feast. The scene belongs to praise poetry, not to a record of an actual meal, but the choice of guests expresses the king’s privileged relationship with the gods. Beer also formed part of ritual offerings and ceremonies.[5]

The Hymn to Ninkasi brings us closer to the work of brewing, placing it in the hands of a goddess. Known from Old Babylonian copies written around 1800 BC, the Sumerian poem addresses Ninkasi as she handles dough, tends malt and works with vessels. Each pair of lines is repeated with her name, giving the preparations the rhythm of a refrain.[2][9]

Its details are practical: a large shovel, an oven, reed mats, a vessel described as making a pleasant sound. Yet when Ninkasi pours the beer, the poem borrows the force of the landscape, “like the onrush of the Tigris and the Euphrates”. Brewing becomes a fitting subject for praise, from the handling of the grain to the flow of the finished drink.[9]

As brewing instructions, however, the hymn leaves important gaps. It does not clearly explain when malt and bappir were brought together or how the sprouting of the grain was stopped. Damerow questions whether the activities even follow the order of a complete brewing process. Difficult terms also leave translators room to read familiar modern techniques into the ancient words. The poem honours the work without giving us enough information to repeat it.[2][9]

The gods could also get into trouble over a drink. In the Sumerian story Inana and Enki, the visiting goddess is welcomed with food, water and beer. As she and Enki drink together, he gives her divine powers associated with human institutions and skills, including kingship and the crafts. When the beer’s effects wear off, he asks his minister where those powers have gone. We cannot tell from the story how strong the beer served in daily life was, but its effects on Enki’s judgement are clear.[10]

In everyday life, taverns were places to drink and do business. An Ur III document cited by Neumann records a credit contract drawn up in a tavern-keeper’s house. Several centuries later, in the eighteenth century BC, the laws associated with the Babylonian king Hammurabi address both the sale of drink and the behaviour of those who gathered to consume it.[5][11]

One provision, conventionally numbered 108, concerns a female tavern-keeper’s acceptance of barley or silver in payment, and the value of the drink supplied in return. Another, number 109, prescribes death for a tavern-keeper who fails to apprehend people plotting criminal acts in her house and bring them to the authorities. These are prescribed penalties, not records of actual prosecutions, but they show a concern with both the terms on which drink was sold and the people who gathered where it was served.[11]

The laws take a female tavern-keeper for granted, making her answerable for both transactions and conduct in her establishment. Alongside earlier accounts that name men and women in beer production, they give us glimpses of who did the work, though not enough to establish how it was divided across the trade.[5][11]

What can these sources tell us?

SourceWhat it can showWhat it cannot establish alone
Accounts[1][2][5]What it can showRecorded quantities, recipients and obligations.What it cannot establish aloneExactly how the beer was brewed or tasted.
Banquet images[8]What it can showHow drinking, service and social rank were represented.What it cannot establish aloneHow typical these scenes were of everyday drinking.
Hymns[2][9]What it can showHow brewing was celebrated and associated with the gods.What it cannot establish aloneA complete, reproducible brewing recipe.
Laws[11]What it can showRules prescribed for sales and conduct in taverns.What it cannot establish aloneWhether, or how often, those rules were enforced.

By the time beer appears in Mesopotamian writing, it is already an established drink. What the records reveal is how it fitted into people’s lives: the work of a brewer, the provisions for a journey and the obligations of a tavern-keeper.

Each source preserves what mattered to its makers, leaving other things unsaid. The scribe who recorded Agu’a’s provisions left no description of the journey or of how the beer tasted. He entered the allowances and totalled them, preserving the name of a traveller beside the food and drink intended to sustain him on his way.
