The Origins of Alcohol · Episode 1.1

Who Invented Alcohol?

The prehistoric origins of drinking

Long before people learned to make alcoholic drinks, fermentation occurred naturally in ripe fruit that had fallen to the ground and in diluted honey. Yeasts convert sugars into alcohol, and our distant ancestors probably first encountered its effects by eating food that had fermented on its own. In time, people learned to encourage this natural process and make fermented drinks deliberately.

Mindrink ScienceEpisode 1.1Research-led history

We do not know who first made a fermented drink, or when or where it happened. No account or recipe records those beginnings, and the drinks themselves have disappeared. Archaeologists can, however, examine traces left in the vessels used to prepare and store them. These remains offer clues to ingredients and methods, while the places where they were found suggest possible settings for drinking.

Tracing the earliest drinks

Alcohol evaporates and organic matter decays, but some traces can survive for thousands of years. Starch grains may remain in a stone mortar, organic acids can be absorbed into a pottery jar, and fragments of plants may persist long after the rest of a drink has gone. Wear on the vessels adds another kind of evidence: a pestle used repeatedly, for example, can leave the stone surface smooth. By comparing these observations, researchers try to work out what the vessels held and how they were used.

Mortars in the Carmel hills, near Galilee, about 13,000 years ago

Near Haifa in northern Israel, Raqefet Cave opens westward in the limestone of Mount Carmel. Hunter-gatherers who had not yet adopted agriculture used the cave during the Late Natufian period, roughly 11,750–9750 BC. Over time, about thirty people were buried in a confined area of its first chamber. Some graves were lined with flowering plants,[6] and animal bones bearing cut marks were found beside the dead.[5]

More than a hundred hollows had been cut into the rocky cave floor and the terrace outside, including mortars and smaller depressions known as cupmarks. Three mortars were selected for detailed study. Two deep mortars, hollowed out of stone blocks, stood close to human remains; one is about 33 centimetres deep and has a hole at its base. The third, in the centre of the chamber, is shaped like a bowl, 18 centimetres deep and 27 centimetres across the rim.[1]

To find out what had been prepared in the mortars, Li Liu, Jiajing Wang and their colleagues examined microscopic plant residues still clinging to the stone. They identified starch from wheat or barley, oats and other plants, including legumes and nutsedge tubers—the starchy underground parts of the plant. Samples taken for comparison from unused surfaces and soil outside the cave contained almost none of these residues.[1]

Some starch grains showed damage resembling that found in barley allowed to sprout and then dried to make malt. Others, from the bowl-shaped mortar, showed changes similar to those observed when the team crushed and fermented cereals in experiments designed to reproduce these processes.[1]

Wear on the stone provided further clues. In the two deeper mortars, the marks could have been caused by plant-fibre baskets rubbing repeatedly against the walls. Those in the bowl-shaped mortar were more consistent with a wooden pestle. Combining this evidence with the starch analysis, Liu’s team reconstructed a possible three-stage brewing process: sprouting and drying grain to make malt, crushing and heating it in water, then allowing it to ferment. The bowl-shaped mortar may therefore have served both for food preparation and for making a low-alcohol drink based on wheat or barley, possibly with other plants added.[1]

If the team is right, some hunter-gatherers were fermenting grain around 11,000 BC, several millennia before cereal domestication in the Near East.[1] Cultivation means growing plants; domestication refers to changes in the plants themselves over generations under human influence.[11]

But the interpretation is disputed. In 2019, the archaeologist David Eitam argued that the narrow, deep mortars were used to mill barley for bread, and that the evidence did not demonstrate beer production.[9] Liu and her colleagues defended their interpretation by drawing together the residues, wear patterns and experimental results.[10] The disagreement concerns what these traces can establish: brewing at Raqefet remains a hypothesis rather than a settled finding.

Rice, honey and fruit at Jiahu, about 9,000 years ago

Several millennia later, and thousands of kilometres farther east, pottery vessels offer evidence of another kind of drink. At Jiahu, an early Neolithic village in China’s Henan province, Patrick McGovern and his colleagues examined organic residues from sixteen vessels dating to roughly 7000–5500 BC. These included narrow-mouthed jars, vessels with tall, flaring necks and perforated basins. Most samples came from the bases, where liquids could have left deposits.[2]

The team’s analysis pointed to a drink made from rice, honey and fruit. The results suggested the presence of rice, the only cereal recovered at the site, while compounds characteristic of beeswax pointed to honey. The fruit was harder to identify. Tartaric acid, detected in the residues, is commonly used as an indicator of grapes in the Near East, but some Chinese hawthorn fruits contain roughly four times as much of it as grapes do. Seeds of both hawthorn and wild grape had been found at Jiahu, leaving open the possibility that either fruit, or a mixture of the two, had been used.[2]

The liquid has disappeared, but the residues suggest that the drink had fermented. Such a mixture could have fermented spontaneously: honey and fruit skins can harbour yeasts, including Saccharomyces cerevisiae, a species used in winemaking. Once the honey was diluted and the fruit crushed, a few warm days could have been enough to start the process.[2]

If McGovern’s interpretation is right, people at Jiahu had learned to start fermentation deliberately by the early Neolithic, around 7000 BC.[2] They were already growing rice, although fishing, hunting and gathering still provided most of their food.[11] Writing would not appear in Mesopotamia for almost another four millennia, around 3200 BC.[12]

Jiahu’s villagers were already growing rice, whereas the people who used the mortars at Raqefet had lived as hunter-gatherers. Whether they brewed remains disputed.[1][9][10] The proposed ingredients differ too: wheat or barley at Raqefet, and rice with honey and fruit at Jiahu.[1][2]

Different ingredients, different preparations

These prehistoric drinks do not always fit neatly into today’s categories of beer, wine, cider and mead. Early preparations probably combined ingredients available locally, without necessarily resembling the recipes we associate with those drinks today. Grain processing illustrates how varied the possibilities could be, and how closely food and drink were connected.

Unlike fruit and honey, cereals contain mainly starch, which must be broken down into sugars that yeasts can turn into alcohol. Ways of preparing grain included roasting, grinding, soaking, cooking it as porridge and allowing it to sprout to produce malt. By learning to process grain in different ways, people could make both bread and fermented drinks.

Bread, beer and the beginnings of farming

In 1953, the archaeologist Robert Braidwood asked what had encouraged people to cultivate cereals: the wish to make bread, or the desire for a fermented drink?[8] The possible brewing traces at Raqefet gave the question renewed relevance.[1]

The mortars also point to a broader range of plant uses. Alongside starch grains showing changes consistent with malting, they contained traces of oats, legumes and nutsedge tubers. Liu’s team therefore regards brewing as one possible technique among many for preparing food in Natufian communities. The desire to make a fermented drink could have been one of several reasons to cultivate cereals in the southern Levant.[1]

People were also making bread before cereals were domesticated. At Shubayqa 1 in eastern Jordan, charred food remains dating to around 12,450 BC have been identified as bread.[7] Those remains are older than the possible brewing traces at Raqefet, but the two sites cannot establish whether bread or beer was made first. They show that people were finding different ways to prepare wild cereals long before domestication.

The debate can be summarised through two explanations and a synthesis informed by these discoveries. The arrows below describe proposed relationships, not a proven sequence of events.

The farming-first hypothesis

agriculture→surplus grain→bread-making→beer-brewing

The hypothesis that beer encouraged farming

desire to make a fermented drink→cereal cultivation→development of agriculture

Evidence for several uses of wild grain

wild cereals→bread-making and possible brewing before domestication

Grape wine in Georgia, about 8,000 years ago

Cereals were not the only ingredients people learned to ferment. In the valleys of the South Caucasus, evidence from the Georgian villages of Gadachrili Gora and Shulaveris Gora points towards grapes. These early Neolithic sites have yielded some of the Near East’s earliest large pottery vessels. A reconstructed jar from nearby Khramis Didi-Gora gives an idea of their scale: almost a metre high and equally wide, it could hold more than 300 litres.[3]

To find out what the jars had held around 6000 BC, McGovern and a team of Georgian and international researchers analysed fragments from comparable vessels. In several samples, concentrations of tartaric acid—the main organic acid in grapes—were much higher than in the surrounding soil. Other acids associated with grapes were also present.[3]

Plant remains added to the chemical evidence. Grape pollen was found in soil layers dating to around 6000 BC but not in samples from the modern surface. Starch and fragments of grape skin were also recovered from a similar jar, and one pottery fragment preserved remains of fruit flies, which are attracted to sugar and alcohol. The researchers inferred that vines probably grew near the villages, perhaps even within the inhabited areas.[3]

Here, the evidence points more specifically to a familiar drink: grape wine. The chemical and botanical findings complement one another, suggesting that people were making wine and storing it in large jars around 6000 BC, even though the liquid has disappeared.[3] Grain at Raqefet, rice with honey and fruit at Jiahu, grapes in Georgia: together, the finds suggest that people learned to make fermented drinks from ingredients available locally.

Drinking and communal life

Residues can suggest what went into a drink and how it was made. To explore the circumstances in which people might have drunk it, archaeologists also examine where the vessels were found and what lay around them. A pot found in a house suggests different possibilities from a mortar beside a grave or a large vat in a gathering place.

Funeral meals at Raqefet, about 13,000 years ago

The burials at Raqefet show considerable care for the dead. In four graves, plants had been laid beneath the bodies, their stems—including those of sage—leaving impressions in a thin layer of mud. The researchers suggest that the plants may have been chosen for their colour and scent. In one grave, the rock had been shaped before the mud lining was laid down. One skeleton was found resting on the rim of a large hollow cut into the rock, identified by the archaeologists as a mortar.[6]

Animal bones from the main burial pit suggest that the animals had been butchered and eaten, rather than buried whole as offerings. The bones bear few of the marks of trampling, burning or gnawing usually seen on household food waste left exposed for long periods. Reuven Yeshurun, Guy Bar-Oz and Dani Nadel suggest that these were the remains of meals shared during funerals. The leftovers may then have been collected and buried with the dead.[5]

Liu and her colleagues connect this evidence of meals with the traces they interpret as brewing. Two of the three mortars they studied were close to human remains, raising the possibility that a fermented drink accompanied the funerals. In their reconstruction, sharing food and drink could have honoured the dead while maintaining ties among the living.[1] The cave’s use as a burial place is well established; whether beer was served during the ceremonies, and what role it played, remain uncertain.

Gatherings at Göbekli Tepe, about 11,600 to 10,200 years ago

On a hill in south-eastern Turkey, T-shaped limestone pillars at Göbekli Tepe form part of monumental structures built roughly 9600–8200 BC. They stand in circles around pairs of taller central pillars; in one enclosure, the central stones rise to about 5.5 metres. Animals are carved into the pillars, while arms and belts on some of the stones give them a stylised human form.[4]

In a 2012 study, Oliver Dietrich and his colleagues interpreted the site as a sanctuary where hunter-gatherers met, rather than a village. They reported no remains of domesticated plants or animals for the phase they discussed.[4]

The earth and debris filling the enclosures contained exceptional quantities of broken gazelle, aurochs and wild-ass bones, far more than usually found in settlements of the same period. Dietrich’s team suggested that these were leftovers from large meals connected with construction work. Groups from the surrounding region might have gathered to eat and help build the monuments. Benches around the enclosures also supported the idea that the spaces could accommodate such gatherings.[4]

In rooms built later, the excavators found six limestone vessels shaped like troughs or vats, some capable of holding up to 160 litres. Grey-black deposits survived on their lower walls. The team tested these for oxalate, a salt that can form when crushed cereals are mixed with hot water during brewing, but the results did not establish that the vessels had contained alcohol. Dietrich and his colleagues nevertheless considered the possibility that a cereal-based drink had been prepared or served in them during gatherings.[4]

The relationship between these meals and the building work could have run both ways. Eating together might strengthen ties, create obligations to help one another or reward work already done. Equally, bringing people together for a ritual building project meant finding ways to feed them. Shared meals could have encouraged participation in the work while meeting the needs of those who carried it out.

Feasts may have enabled monuments

shared meals, perhaps with alcohol→social bonds→cooperation→collective labour→monumental construction

Construction may have prompted communal meals

ritual obligations and construction→demand for labour→organised communal meals

The activities could reinforce one another

gathering↔ritual↔shared meals↔cooperation↔monumental construction

In this interpretation, alcohol may have played a part in these gatherings, but it cannot by itself explain the emergence of Neolithic societies.[4]

Beginnings we cannot fully recover

We still cannot say who first made alcoholic drinks, or where. According to Liu’s disputed interpretation, hunter-gatherers at Raqefet may have made a fermented drink from grain around 11,000 BC.[1][9][10] Jiahu suggests a rice, honey and fruit drink around 7000 BC, while the Georgian finds point more specifically to grape wine around 6000 BC.[2][3] These dates tell us how old the surviving traces are, not when people first learned to make each kind of drink. Nor do they establish whether the techniques developed independently or spread between communities.

The vessels found near Raqefet’s burials and in the gathering spaces of Göbekli Tepe also suggest that some drinks may have been shared at funerals or communal meals. That does not allow us to reconstruct those events in detail. The known sites are places where traces have survived and been found, not necessarily where people first made fermented drinks. Other communities may also have made and shared fermented drinks, leaving traces that have since disappeared or have yet to be discovered.

More detailed accounts become available with the emergence of writing, conventionally taken to mark the transition from prehistory to history. The next part of the story takes us to Mesopotamia, one of the earliest centres of civilisation, where writing appeared around 3200 BC.[12] Clay tablets recording quantities of beer distributed to workers offer a closer view of the drink’s place in daily life.[13]
