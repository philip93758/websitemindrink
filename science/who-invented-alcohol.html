The Origins of Alcohol · Episode 1.1

Who Invented Alcohol?

The prehistoric origins of drinking

Fermentation is far older than humanity. Wherever yeast encountered sugar—in split fruit, diluted honey or wet grain left in the sun—it consumed it and produced ethanol as a by-product. People therefore met alcohol long before they learned to make it: as a sweet, faintly fizzy and potentially dangerous feature of the natural world.

No prehistoric drink has survived for us to taste, and no name or recipe records its maker. Archaeology can nevertheless approach a more revealing question by identifying the period in which people appear to have begun producing fermented beverages deliberately.

The evidence revolves around three questions: when deliberate production began, what those early drinks contained, and what place they occupied in prehistoric life.

When did humans start making alcohol?

Ethanol evaporates and organic matter decays, leaving archaeologists to work with indirect traces: starch trapped in a stone mortar, acids absorbed by a ceramic wall, fragments of plants and the polished surface left by repeated use of a pestle. These objects provide the material record from which the earliest history of drinking must be reconstructed.

A cave on Mount Carmel, about 13,000 years ago

Raqefet Cave opens westward from the limestone of Mount Carmel, in present-day Israel. During the Late Natufian period, roughly 13,700 to 11,700 years ago, it was used by hunter-gatherers who had not yet adopted agriculture. In a confined area of the first chamber they repeatedly buried their dead, eventually placing about thirty people in the rocky floor. Some graves were lined with flowering plants,[6] and butchered animal bones were deposited alongside them.[5]

More than a hundred hollows had been cut into the cave floor and terrace around these burials, among them mortars and cupmarks worked into the bedrock. Two deep boulder mortars stood beside human remains; one reaches about 33 centimetres in depth and has a hole at its base. A third, bowl-shaped mortar in the centre of the chamber measures 18 centimetres deep and 27 centimetres across the rim.[1]

Li Liu, Jiajing Wang and their colleagues examined the three mortars for microscopic plant remains and patterns of wear. Their samples contained starches from wheat or barley, oats, legumes, sedge tubers and other plants, whereas control scrapings from unused surfaces and soil outside the cave were almost empty. Under the microscope, some cereal starches resembled malted barley; others, taken from the bowl-shaped mortar, matched patterns produced when the researchers mashed and fermented grain experimentally.[1]

The wear inside the deep mortars is consistent with fibre baskets being moved repeatedly up and down, while the bowl-shaped vessel bears marks associated with pounding by a wooden pestle. Liu’s team reconstructs a three-stage process familiar from later brewing: grain was sprouted and dried to make malt, crushed and warmed in water, and finally left to ferment. The bowl mortar probably served several purposes, including the preparation of plant foods and a low-alcohol drink based on wheat or barley, perhaps supplemented with other plants.[1]

If this interpretation is correct, hunter-gatherers were fermenting grain several thousand years before domesticated cereals appeared in the Near East. The evidence does not establish that the world’s first beer was invented on Mount Carmel; it shows that three mortars in a cemetery preserve traces consistent with brewing.

Jars in Henan, about 9,000 years ago

Several thousand years later, and thousands of kilometres farther east, a different form of evidence appears at Jiahu, an early Neolithic village in China’s Henan province. Patrick McGovern and his colleagues extracted organic residues from sixteen pottery vessels, including narrow-mouthed storage jars, high-necked jars with flaring rims and perforated basins. Most samples came from the bases, where liquids would have lingered, and thirteen of the sixteen shared a similar chemical profile. The vessels date from about 9,000 to 7,500 years ago.[2]

Although the alcohol had disappeared, its chemical signature offered the outline of a recipe. The results pointed to rice, the only cereal recovered at Jiahu, while hydrocarbons characteristic of beeswax indicated honey and tartaric acid suggested fruit. In the Near East, tartaric acid is a strong marker for grapes; in China the interpretation is less straightforward because hawthorn contains roughly four times as much of it. Since both hawthorn and wild-grape seeds have been found at Jiahu, McGovern concludes that the vessels held a mixed fermented drink made from rice, honey and one or both fruits.[2]

Fermentation remains an inference rather than a surviving liquid, but the process would not have been difficult to encounter. Saccharomyces cerevisiae, the yeast involved in winemaking, occurs in honey and on the skins of sweet fruit; once honey is diluted and fruit crushed, fermentation can begin within a few warm days. McGovern argues that the people of Jiahu had learned to reproduce and contain that process in jars.

Jiahu should not be understood as a continuation of Raqefet. Rather, it demonstrates that sophisticated fermentation was being developed independently, far from the Near East, by about 9,000 years ago. The history of alcohol therefore appears to have had several centres, as different populations learned to reproduce the same naturally occurring microbial process.

By the early Neolithic, and perhaps several millennia earlier, people had moved beyond the accidental discovery of fermented fruit and were learning to create the necessary conditions deliberately.

What were the first alcoholic drinks?

Modern categories such as beer, wine, cider and mead are of limited use in prehistory. The earliest fermented beverages were probably mixtures assembled from whatever ingredients were available and capable of fermenting; standardised recipes belong to a later period.

Unlike fruit and honey, which already contain accessible sugars, grain consists largely of starch and must be processed before fermentation can begin. Prehistoric communities could roast, grind, soak or cook it as porridge, allow it to germinate into malt, or ferment it. Bread was one outcome of this expanding knowledge of cereals, but it was not the only one.

Beer before bread? Evidence from 14,400 to 13,000 years ago

In 1953, the archaeologist Robert Braidwood asked whether the desire for a fermented cereal drink, rather than the demand for bread, might have encouraged people to cultivate grain.[8] The question has endured because the evidence from Raqefet places possible cereal fermentation among hunter-gatherers before the appearance of domesticated cereals in the Near East. The debate is better represented as three competing propositions:

Farming came first

agriculture→surplus grain→bread→beer

Beer encouraged farming

demand for fermented grain→cereal cultivation→agriculture

The evidence supports experimentation

processing wild cereals→bread and brewing before domestication

The mortars support the third proposition rather than a simple beer-first story. Alongside malt-like cereal starches they retained traces of oats, legumes and sedge tubers, suggesting that brewing was one of several ways in which Natufian communities processed plants and perhaps one of several incentives to cultivate cereals in the southern Levant.[1] At Shubayqa 1 in eastern Jordan, another Natufian site, charred food remains about 14,400 years old have been identified as bread.[7] These finds come from different places and do not establish a universal order of invention. They show instead that baking and brewing formed part of a broader period of experimentation with wild cereals before the first fields.

A jar the size of a person, about 8,000 years ago

Roughly another millennium later, the evidence leads to the valleys of the South Caucasus. At the early Neolithic villages of Gadachrili Gora and Shulaveris Gora in Georgia, some of the Near East’s earliest large ceramic vessels were being produced. A reconstructed jar from nearby Khramis Didi-Gora is almost a metre high and equally wide, with a capacity of more than 300 litres. When McGovern and a large Georgian and international team sampled the clay of comparable jars, several sherds contained far more tartaric acid—the principal organic acid in grapes—than the surrounding soil, together with other acids associated with the fruit.[3]

Botanical evidence supports the chemical analysis. Grape pollen occurs in the Neolithic layers but not in the modern topsoil, while starch and fragments of grape skin were found with a similar jar. One sherd even contained fruit flies, which are attracted to sugar and alcohol. Grapevines appear to have grown close to the villages, perhaps within them, leading the team to conclude that grape wine was produced and stored in these exceptionally large vessels.[3]

Of all the beverages in this account, the Georgian example is the first that a modern drinker would immediately recognise: grapes processed in quantity and stored in vessels designed for the purpose. This was not the next step in a linear progression from mixed drinks to beer and then wine, but a separate development in another region and millennium. What Jiahu and the Georgian sites share is evidence of increasing control over fermentation.

Why did prehistoric humans drink?

The honest answer is that we do not know. Residues can indicate what a vessel once contained, but they cannot explain why somebody chose to drink from it. Archaeologists must therefore interpret the wider context, whether a house, a cemetery or a hill where large groups gathered to build on a monumental scale.

Drinking with the dead at Raqefet, about 13,000 years ago

In four graves at Raqefet, bodies were laid on a thin mud lining that preserved impressions of plant stems, including sage, so that colour and scent formed part of the burial. In one case the bedrock had been shaped before the lining was added, and one skeleton was found resting on the rim of a large mortar cut into the rock.[6]

The animal bones in the principal burial hollow were the remains of meals rather than intact carcasses offered as grave goods. They had been butchered and consumed, yet they lacked the accumulated trampling, burning and gnawing associated with long-term domestic refuse. Reuven Yeshurun, Guy Bar-Oz and Dani Nadel therefore interpret them as the remains of brief, intermittent funerary feasts that were collected and buried with the dead.[5]

The traces interpreted as brewing belong to this same setting, since two of the analysed mortars stood beside human remains. Liu consequently argues that a fermented cereal drink formed part of the mortuary rites, serving both the dead and a living community that had to preserve its cohesion.[1] The flowers and animal bones establish the funerary setting independently; the association between the starches and a ritual drink rests above all on the location of the mortars.

A hill of pillars, about 11,600 to 10,200 years ago

At Göbekli Tepe, a hilltop site in south-eastern Turkey used roughly 11,600 to 10,200 years ago, the relationship between gathering, ritual and drink appears on a monumental scale. Rather than a village, the excavators interpret the site as a sanctuary in which hunter-gatherers arranged circles of T-shaped limestone pillars around two taller central stones. Carvings of animals, arms and belts give some pillars the appearance of stylised human figures, while the central pair in one enclosure rises to about 5.5 metres. No domesticated plants or animals have been identified from this phase.[4]

The fill contained far more smashed bone from gazelle, aurochs and wild ass than is normally found at contemporary settlements, and it does not resemble ordinary household refuse. Oliver Dietrich and his colleagues interpret the material as evidence of feasts connected with collective labour: people came from a wider region, ate together and helped raise the stones. Benches around the enclosures suggest that the architecture itself was designed for gatherings.[4]

Six limestone troughs and barrel-shaped vessels were found in later rooms, some with capacities of up to 160 litres and grey-black residues on their lower walls. The evidence for beer is less secure: tests sought oxalate, a salt that can form during the mashing of grain. Dietrich suggests that a fermented cereal drink may have been prepared or served at these gatherings, perhaps using malt brought from elsewhere.[4] Even if beer was present, it would have been only one component of a much larger social system.

Göbekli Tepe matters to the history of drinking because it exposes a difficult question of cause and effect. Feasts can create obligations, affirm membership and reward labour, but large ritual projects can equally generate the need to organise food, drink and workers. The competing interpretations are better shown together:

Feasts enabled monuments

feasting, perhaps with alcohol→social bonds→cooperation→collective labour→monumental construction

Monuments generated feasts

ritual obligations and construction→demand for labour→organised feasting

The relationship was reciprocal

gathering↔ritual↔feasting↔cooperation↔collective construction

This synthesis assigns alcohol a possible supporting role rather than treating it as the engine of social change. Dietrich and his colleagues reject the claim that people lived by beer alone, yet fermented beverages may have contributed to gatherings in which increasingly large and organised groups learned to cooperate. The defensible question is therefore not whether alcohol created the Neolithic, but whether drinking formed part of the communal practices through which such societies were built.

Alcohol before civilisation

The available evidence identifies no single inventor. Mortars from a cemetery point to possible brewing among hunter-gatherers about 13,000 years ago; chemical residues document a mixed fermented drink in China about 9,000 years ago; and large Georgian vessels contained something recognisably close to grape wine about 8,000 years ago. These were parallel experiments rather than stages spreading from a single point of origin, and their products did not yet correspond neatly to the drinks named in a modern bar.

Purpose is harder to establish than ingredients or technique. The sites preserving early traces of alcohol also contain graves, meals and architecture intended for gatherings, which has led researchers to examine ritual, funerary practice, feasting and social bonds. Those contexts provide the strongest interpretation available, although this is also the point at which material evidence gives way to a more uncertain argument.

The nature of the record changes in Mesopotamia, where microscopic residues and spatial context are joined by something unprecedented: written accounts of beer.
