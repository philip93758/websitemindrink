The Origins of Alcohol · Episode 1.4

Greece: Wine and the Human Mind

Mindrink ScienceEpisode 1.4Research-led history

Agathon is hosting a drinking party in Athens. Before his guests can make speeches about love, they need to agree on the drinking.

Several are still suffering from the previous night's celebrations. One of the guests, Pausanias, asks how they can make this evening easier on themselves. Aristophanes, another guest, agrees that another heavy session would be unwelcome. Eryximachus, a physician, recommends avoiding deep drinking, particularly while they are still recovering. The company settles on a rule: everyone may drink as much or as little as he pleases. They will spend the evening in conversation.[1]

This is the opening of the philosopher Plato's Symposium, a fourth-century BC dialogue set at an earlier gathering in Athens. Its guests are preparing to discuss desire, beauty and human nature while sharing a drink capable of changing their feelings and judgement. Keeping the conversation going requires some agreement about the cups.

How did Greek writers understand—and try to use—wine’s power to change thoughts, feelings and behaviour?

Their accounts concern practical capacities: judgement, memory and command of one's actions. Wine could unsettle those capacities, yet its changes were also sought. Poets welcomed emotional relief, companions negotiated a pleasant degree of intoxication, and some philosophical writers considered whether drinking could reveal a person's character or be used to help educate it.

These voices belong to different periods and settings. Homeric epic, archaic lyric, fifth-century history and fourth-century dialogues offer different kinds of access to the subject. Together, they allow us to follow a recurring concern: what changes when a person drinks, and what should people make of those changes?

I. Wine and its power over the human mind

Changing emotions

Around 600 BC, the poet Alcaeus of Lesbos urged a companion to answer misfortune with wine and drunkenness. Grieving would achieve nothing, he argued. His advice is blunt: “the best cure is to have wine brought and get drunk.” A different fragment calls for intoxication because Myrsilus, his political enemy, is dead.[2]

The two invitations begin from different emotions. One seeks relief from something painful. The other summons companions to celebrate welcome news. The second does not show wine producing the original joy: the death has already supplied its occasion. Drinking belongs to the response. A person might therefore seek wine to escape a painful feeling or to dwell more intensely in a welcome one.

The poems survive as fragments quoted centuries later by the writer Athenaeus, who collects them to demonstrate Alcaeus' enthusiasm for drinking in different circumstances. We hear invitations selected for a later argument, rather than an uninterrupted account of the poet's life. Even so, their appeal is explicit: wine offers a way of responding to experience. The change it promises is part of what makes it worth calling for.[2]

Loosening the tongue

These invitations concern the feelings a drinker hopes to change. Speech presents a further question: when someone says too much and blames the wine, has he lost restraint or found a useful excuse? Homer's Odyssey makes that distinction matter.

In Book 14, the poem's hero, Odysseus (also known as Ulysses), has returned to Ithaca in disguise. He is staying with the swineherd Eumaeus, who does not recognise him. Odysseus wants a warm cloak for the night. Before he speaks, the narrator tells us that he is testing whether his host will provide one.[3]

Odysseus begins by saying that wine makes even a wise man sing, dance and speak when silence would be wiser. He then tells of a freezing night near Troy, casting himself as a different soldier who has no cloak. In the tale, that soldier asks Odysseus—the very man now telling the story in disguise—for help. Odysseus calls for a messenger to the ships; a third soldier volunteers, throws off his cloak and runs, leaving it for the cold man to lie under.

Odysseus ends the tale by bringing it back to his present need. If he were still young and strong, he says, one of Eumaeus's men would give him a cloak. The appeal works: Eumaeus praises the story and covers his guest with a thick cloak for the night.[3]

Wine is Odysseus' stated reason for talking; obtaining a cloak is his purpose. The scene therefore complicates the idea that drink simply makes people lose control of their words. A speaker can draw on that belief while making a carefully judged request.

Losing judgement

Odysseus remains in command of his request while invoking wine's power to loosen restraint. That power could also be invoked to explain disturbing behaviour whose cause was disputed. Herodotus, a Greek historian writing in the fifth century BC, preserves such a dispute in his Histories. He describes how Cleomenes, a king of Sparta, became violent, was confined by his relatives and died after injuring himself.[4]

According to the Spartans, Cleomenes had learned to drink unmixed wine, meaning wine without added water, through close contact with Scythian envoys visiting Sparta. This habit, they said, caused his madness. Other accounts connected his fate with offences involving sacred places or religious authority. Herodotus himself favoured the explanation that Cleomenes' end was punishment for his actions against his fellow king Demaratus.[4]

In the Spartan account, the foreign custom helps explain how their king became disordered. The story joins a claim about wine's effects to a judgement about the company and habits he had adopted.

Herodotus' account cannot establish a modern diagnosis. Its importance lies in the competing explanations it preserves. Wine was sufficiently associated with a loss of reason to make the Spartan account intelligible, but that account did not settle the matter. Different tellers interpreted the same fate through different concerns. Recognising that wine could disturb a person left considerable room for disagreement about when it had done so, and why.

II. The social art of drinking

A gathering with conventions

Changes in speech and judgement could also affect the people around the drinker. The symposion, or drinking party, brought that difficulty into a shared setting. Among Greek male elites, guests reclined on couches, shared wine and took part in conversation, poetry, music and entertainment. Archaeologists have identified rooms arranged for such gatherings. Surviving vessels provide examples of the drinking equipment, and some carry painted scenes of the gatherings themselves.[5]

A large mixing bowl, the krater, stood at the centre of wine service. Wine and water were combined before the mixture was distributed in cups. A drinking leader could help determine its strength and the course of the occasion. Decisions about drinking were therefore partly decisions about a shared arrangement.[5]

An Athenian cup from about 480 BC gives a visual example of that shared service: its exterior shows a symposium with mixing and serving equipment. Inside are a flute-playing satyr and a maenad, followers of Dionysus, the god of wine.[6]

This elite setting represents one important drinking environment. Wine also belonged to meals, work, trade and offerings.[3][14][15] Within the symposium, however, its effects were experienced among companions who could encourage, judge or challenge one another. How someone drank affected the company around him.

That shared experience depended on people of unequal standing. Enslaved attendants, musicians and other entertainers helped sustain the gathering. The reclining guests enjoyed privileges that some of those serving or entertaining them did not share.[5] Wine service also affected the pace of drinking. In a fourth-century BC dialogue also called the Symposium, the writer Xenophon shows how that pace could complicate the guests' attempt to maintain their desired mood.[7]

Finding the right amount

In Xenophon's scene, the philosopher Socrates approves of wine because it relieves grief and encourages kindly feelings. He compares its effects to watering plants: a flood makes them collapse, while suitable watering allows them to flourish.[7]

For the guests, immense draughts would soon leave both body and mind reeling. Small cups, offered frequently, might instead encourage a playful mood while allowing sensible speech. The desired experience includes a change in feeling. Socrates wants the company to enjoy the wine and remain capable of enjoying one another.[7]

The guests approve the proposal, but Philip, the jester, adds an amendment: the servants should circulate the cups with increasing speed. The wine-pourers promptly begin driving the cups around faster.[7]

Philip's amendment exposes a gap between the guests' agreed aim and their arrangements. Small cups alone cannot secure the desired mood if the servants keep accelerating the rounds.

Personal choice and the pressure of company

An agreeable mood for the company does not mean that every guest wants the same amount of wine. Verses collected under the name of the poet Theognis approach drinking through the treatment of companions. This composite body of poetry, known as the Theognidean collection, cannot be treated as a securely dated statement by one author. In one poem, no unwilling guest should be detained, no person wishing to stay sent away, and no sleeper needlessly woken. Let those who want wine receive it. The speaker himself has reached a pleasurable point between sobriety and excessive drunkenness and intends to go home.[8]

The poem also describes the difficulty of keeping to such a decision. Cups arrive for friendship, for a wager, for a libation to the gods. Each offers another reason to drink, and refusing becomes difficult. Someone who understands his limit may nevertheless find himself repeatedly exceeding it.[8]

The recommendations concern the character of the gathering as much as the individual. Respecting different wishes could help preserve enjoyment. Companions could also make an intended stopping point harder to maintain.

The speaker's concern for choice also has social limits: he warns his companion against surrendering to appetite, presenting the hired day labourer as someone who does just that. The advice carries elite prejudice into its account of self-command.[8]

A drinker ready to leave still faces the next cup offered in friendship or for the gods. Knowing how much he wants does not settle how much he will drink. And companions who watch one another drink may judge more than the amount consumed: they may judge the person.

III. Wine, character and philosophical inquiry

Does wine reveal the person?

Another poem in the same Theognidean collection gives drinking a further use. Skilled people test gold and silver through fire; wine can disclose a person's mind. The verses immediately complicate the comparison by describing the disgrace that can overtake someone previously considered wise.[9]

Another short poem turns to a drinker's own predicament. His head is heavy, the room seems to move, and he doubts his command of his judgement. He considers getting up to discover whether wine controls his feet as well. What troubles him is the prospect of doing something foolish and having to bear the resulting reproach.[9]

The image of testing precious metal promises access to a quality beneath the surface. Yet the surrounding verses allow another reading: drinking changes the very capacities through which a person ordinarily acts. The formerly sensible man may become foolish. Revelation and transformation sit close together.

That ambiguity matters whenever companions judge one another through drinking. A person may appear to disclose a hidden disposition while also behaving under changed conditions. The collection does not resolve this into a single theory. Its different voices make wine both a means of scrutiny and something that can unsettle the person being scrutinised.

Alcibiades interrupts the philosophers

At Agathon's house, Plato gives these questions a vivid human form. The discussion of love is interrupted by the arrival of Alcibiades, the prominent Athenian political figure[13], already drunk and wearing a garland. He soon objects to the company's apparent sobriety and appoints himself to direct the drinking.[10]

Invited to praise Socrates, Alcibiades describes his fascination with him and the humiliation of a failed seduction. Before recounting the most intimate part, he says that he could hardly tell it sober and invokes wine's truth-telling power. His drunkenness becomes a reason to disclose an experience he would otherwise hesitate to describe.[10]

His admiration is inseparable from frustration. Socrates makes him recognise the gap between the life he leads and the attention he ought to give his own character. Away from Socrates, the desire for public approval regains its hold. In his presence, Alcibiades feels ashamed. The speech makes a failure to live by one's acknowledged judgements painfully personal.[10]

Socrates hears another purpose. He tells Agathon, their host, that Alcibiades is trying to set them against one another: Alcibiades wants Socrates' exclusive devotion while keeping Agathon for himself.[10] On this reading, the confession is also an attempt to control the affections of the men listening.

The scene recalls Odysseus' appeal for a cloak: wine can explain why someone speaks without explaining everything he wants his words to do. Alcibiades exposes desires and shame, yet Socrates questions the purpose of that exposure. Alcibiades also describes Socrates as exceptionally resistant to wine. Drinking gives the company material for judging character, while leaving them to interpret both the speaker's motives and the drink's effects.[10]

The evening's original arrangement breaks down when more revellers arrive and, according to the dialogue, everyone is compelled to drink large quantities of wine. By dawn, only a few remain awake. Socrates is still conversing while his companions struggle to follow. Plato lets the reader observe how an agreement, individual desires and the arrival of new drinkers can change the gathering.[1]

Could intoxication educate the soul?

The scenes at Agathon's house leave us asking how to interpret behaviour under wine. Plato's Laws poses a further question: could the changes brought by wine be deliberately used to educate character? The dialogue's Athenian speaker begins with an account of its disturbing effects: feelings become more intense, while heavy drinking weakens perception, memory and understanding. His companions therefore have good reason to question any defence of intoxication.[11]

The Athenian asks them to consider training. Courage develops through encounters with fear; self-command might likewise require encounters with pleasures that encourage shamelessness. He imagines a potion that would induce fear and allow a person to practise resisting it. That potion is explicitly hypothetical. Wine offers the opposite kind of challenge: it encourages confidence, which can grow into overconfidence.[11]

Under supervision, he argues, drinking might let people observe how a companion responds to this change and practise resisting its worse tendencies. The proposal turns a familiar difficulty into a possible exercise. Knowing the rule while entirely untempted would be different from maintaining it when confidence rises and restraint becomes harder.

This is civic education: the Athenian wants to shape the dispositions of people who must live together under laws.

The proposed drinking party has demanding conditions. The Athenian requires calm, sober supervisors, capable of directing people whose confidence is growing. He also proposes different permissions and restrictions according to age. The passage offers an imagined system to be assessed, rather than a record of Greek society implementing it.[12]

Wine could enter education by another route as well. In Book II of the Laws, the difficulty is getting older men to participate in singing: they may be reluctant or embarrassed. Wine, presented as Dionysus' gift, could ease their cares and make their dispositions more flexible, like iron softened in a fire. They might become willing to sing before a circle of friends. The Athenian wants these men to learn to choose suitable music and, through their singing, guide the young towards an appreciation of good character. Wine would help secure their participation; the educational work would depend on what they learn and sing.[12]

These proposed uses give alteration two educational roles: a challenge through which to practise restraint, and a way to draw reluctant men into singing. In each case, someone must decide what sort of character the exercise should encourage. The Athenian assigns that authority to sober supervisors and, ultimately, the lawgiver. Wine's temporary weakening of self-command could, on this argument, help cultivate it; easing inhibition could help secure participation. The claimed benefit depends on who directs the drinking, for what purpose and within what limits.

Wine and the limits of reason

Greek writers gave wine's changes several meanings. Alcaeus could welcome intoxication in distress or celebration. Homer made loosened speech useful to an exceptionally calculating speaker. Drinking advice addressed the pressures companions placed on one another. Plato's Laws proposed putting wine's effects to educational use.

These accounts share an interest in capacities that ordinary life requires: speaking appropriately, judging a situation, managing desire and responding to others. Wine could alter those capacities while offering pleasures people actively sought. Understanding the change did not settle its value. Nor did observing someone drink always distinguish a disposition being revealed from a capacity being altered.

At the end of Plato's Symposium, Socrates, Aristophanes and Agathon are still passing a large cup as they talk. The other two fall asleep; Socrates leaves and goes about his day.[1] The shared wine has not made the men alike. Socrates' exceptional composure stands out at the close of an evening in which the guests have had many occasions to judge one another. Yet the dialogue leaves open how much the drinkers' behaviour revealed about their character and how much it owed to the wine.
